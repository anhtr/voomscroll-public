VoomScroll shows Vietnamese drawn from open dictionaries, sentence corpora
and public-domain literature. Every source is credited below, with a note on
what was changed on the way in.

# Words and definitions

English Wiktionary. Definitions, parts of speech and usage notes are
extracted from the enwiktionary XML dump.

Changed: wikitext markup stripped and re-rendered as HTML; entries reduced to
their Vietnamese section; some entries filtered out entirely.

https://en.wiktionary.org

# Poetry

Truyện Kiều, by Nguyễn Du (d. 1820). Public domain. The Vietnamese text comes
from Vietnamese Wikisource.

https://vi.wikisource.org/wiki/Truyện_Kiều

Lục Vân Tiên, by Nguyễn Đình Chiểu (d. 1888). Public domain. The Vietnamese
text comes from Vietnamese Wikisource (the 2,082-line quốc ngữ edition),
cross-checked against the 1916 chữ Nôm edition. Every place the two editions
differ was adjudicated and then reviewed by hand; no correction was applied,
and the text stands as transcribed. The judgements are recorded in
dataraw/verse-luc-van-tien/.

https://vi.wikisource.org/wiki/Lục_Vân_Tiên_(bản_Quốc_ngữ_2082_câu)

The English translations of both poems were written for this project by an AI
model, composed directly from the Vietnamese rather than adapted from any
published translation. No human Vietnamese speaker has reviewed them.

# Prose

Nửa chừng xuân, by Khái Hưng (1934). Public domain in Vietnam since 1998, and
never restored in the United States: it had already expired at source before
either date that could have restored it. Text from Vietnamese Wikisource,
verified against the transcription at isach.info. Wikisource is the base here
because isach does not carry the novel's own chapter division - it runs two
chapters together and invents a title for the untitled opening one. Two long
passages the two transcriptions disagree about were adjudicated by hand and
resolved in favour of Wikisource, which carries text isach drops; four
mechanical transcription faults were corrected, and the reasoning for each is
recorded in dataraw/prose/khai_hung_nua_chung_xuan/.

https://vi.wikisource.org/wiki/Nửa_chừng_xuân

Số đỏ, by Vũ Trọng Phụng (serialised 1936, first book edition 1938). Public
domain in Vietnam since 1989. Text from isach.info, verified against the
independent transcription at book.sachgiai.com. Neither source is backed by
page scans, so the two transcriptions audit each other; every place they
disagreed at length was adjudicated by hand, and the corrections applied are
recorded in dataraw/prose/vu_trong_phung_so_do/.

https://isach.info/story.php?story=so_do__vu_trong_phung

Chí Phèo, by Nam Cao (1941). Public domain in Vietnam since 2002. Text from
isach.info, verified word-for-word against the Vietnamese Wikisource
transcription of the 1941 first edition.

Lão Hạc, by Nam Cao (1943), first published in Tiểu thuyết thứ Bảy, số 484,
23-10-1943. Public domain in Vietnam since 2002. Text from isach.info,
verified word-for-word against the Vietnamese Wikisource transcription. No
scan-backed edition of this story exists on Wikisource, so both witnesses are
typed transcriptions rather than proofread page images.

Sống mòn, by Nam Cao, written in 1944 as Chết mòn and first published
posthumously in 1956. Public domain in Vietnam since 2002, and never restored
in the United States: the term runs from the author's death in 1951, so the
late publication does not move it. Text from Vietnamese Wikisource, pinned to
one revision per part for all twenty parts, and verified word-for-word against
the transcription at isach.info. Wikisource is the base because its subpages
carry the novel's part division and isach carries none. No scan-backed edition
of this novel exists on Wikisource, so both witnesses are typed transcriptions
rather than proofread page images, and they agree so closely (99.99% of words)
that the pairing establishes that no passage is missing rather than that the
wording is clean. Forty-one transcription faults that both copies carry were
corrected by hand after an end-of-run adjudication that no human has yet
reviewed; the reasoning for each, the twenty-seven readings deliberately left
as printed, and the pinned revisions are recorded in
dataraw/prose/nam_cao_song_mon/.

https://vi.wikisource.org/wiki/Sống_mòn

Hà Nội băm sáu phố phường, by Thạch Lam (1943). Public domain in Vietnam since
1992. Text from Vietnamese Wikisource, verified against the transcription at
sachhayonline.com.

Gió lạnh đầu mùa, by Thạch Lam (1937). Public domain in Vietnam since 1992.
Text from Vietnamese Wikisource, verified word-for-word against the
transcription at isach.info. No scan-backed edition of this story exists on
Wikisource, and the two witnesses are demonstrably descended from a common
digital ancestor, so the pairing establishes that no passage is missing rather
than that the wording is clean.

Dưới bóng hoàng lan, by Thạch Lam (1942), first published in the collection
Sợi tóc, NXB Đời Nay. Public domain in Vietnam since 1992, and never restored
in the United States. Text from Vietnamese Wikisource, pinned to revision
205716, and verified word-for-word against the transcription at isach.info. No
scan-backed edition of this story exists on Wikisource; the two witnesses are
demonstrably descended from a common digital ancestor, and a third site,
sachhayonline.com, carries that same transcription token for token, so the
pairing establishes that no passage is missing rather than that the wording is
clean. Two transcription faults that every copy in that ancestry carries were
corrected by hand, both corroborated by a further transcription found outside
it; the reasoning for those, and for the readings deliberately left alone, is
recorded in dataraw/prose/thach_lam_duoi_bong_hoang_lan/.

https://vi.wikisource.org/wiki/Dưới_bóng_hoàng_lan

Tuyên ngôn toàn thế giới về nhân quyền — the Universal Declaration of Human
Rights, UN General Assembly resolution 217 A (III). The Vietnamese text is the
translation published by the Office of the UN High Commissioner for Human
Rights, as part of that official record.

https://www.ohchr.org/en/human-rights/universal-declaration/translations/vietnamese

# Sino-Vietnamese roots

English Wiktionary, under CC BY-SA 4.0 as above: Han character pages, word
etymologies and compound lists.

The Unicode Unihan database: Hán-Việt, Mandarin, Japanese and Korean readings,
and English glosses for characters Wiktionary leaves unglossed. The required
notice is at the end of this page.

https://www.unicode.org/reports/tr38/

hvdic.thivien.net, Thi Viện's Hán-Nôm dictionary, and HVDic, its desktop
edition: used as a reference for which characters carry more than one
Hán-Việt reading, and for which derived words belong to which reading. The
site states no licence for this material. Only words that also have their own
Wiktionary entry are shown.

https://hvdic.thivien.net

Changed, across all three: readings reconciled between sources; one card built
per character and reading rather than per character; derived words filtered to
ones this app can gloss or define.

CC-CEDICT was evaluated as a possible fourth source, to fill in characters
and roots the above leave unglossed. dataraw/root-sino/CEDICT_EVAL.md quotes
some of its glosses to compare against Wiktionary's. No pipeline data uses
it — the evaluation found the accuracy trade-off wasn't worth it.

https://cc-cedict.org

CC-CEDICT is used under CC BY-SA 4.0, the same licence as Wiktionary.

# Loanwords

English Wiktionary, under CC BY-SA 4.0 as above: the borrowing templates in
Vietnamese etymology sections, which name the donor language and the source
word, and the definitions of both the Vietnamese word and the source word —
the latter taken from the source word's own entry, in its own language's
section.

Changed: each borrowing paired with the definition of the etymology it belongs
to rather than the first one on the page; the two definitions checked against
each other, and a card kept only where the meaning is pinned down; source
words in Khmer, Korean, Russian, Thai, Lao, Tibetan and Sanskrit romanised,
which Wiktionary renders from a template rather than storing; words that are
not written as Vietnamese, and proper nouns, dropped.

# Classifiers

English Wiktionary, under CC BY-SA 4.0 as above: the cls= argument on
Vietnamese noun entries, which names the classifier or classifiers a noun
takes, and each classifier's own Classifier definition — its semantic rule —
together with the usage notes, synonyms, chữ Hán spellings and example phrases
on the classifier's page.

Changed: each cls= argument paired with the definition from the same
part-of-speech block rather than the first one on the page; nouns Wiktionary
lists without defining dropped; a rule inherited where one classifier is
recorded as a synonym of another; the noun-to-classifier mapping inverted to
list example nouns under each classifier.

Not from Wiktionary: 15 of the 2,781 classifier cards, and 39 of the
classifier rules, were written for this repository rather than extracted. They
fill two gaps. First, four classifiers — đôi, cặp, kỳ and đoàn — carried a rule
but governed no nouns at all, so the card that would teach them could never be
built. Second, 59 classifiers appeared on cards with no definition anywhere in
Wiktionary, so the card could state a pairing without explaining it. Those records carry "source": "curated" (or
"curated-gloss", where only the English definition was replaced) in
data/classifiers/**, and are not under CC BY-SA. They were written by Claude
Opus 5 and reviewed by a Vietnamese speaker, which cut the pairings from 182 to
11 and kept the rules as written; see dataraw/classifier/curated/README.md for
what that review covered.

# Minimal pairs

English Wiktionary, under CC BY-SA 4.0 as above: the definitions of
Vietnamese monosyllables, regrouped into two kinds of set — words sharing a
spelling but differing only in tone mark (ma/mà/má/mã/mả/mạ), and words
sharing a tone but differing in one vowel letter within a, ă, â; e, ê; o, ô,
ơ; or u, ư (máy/mấy) — that Wiktionary itself does not group.

Changed: nothing about the definitions themselves — every gloss ships
unedited and unabridged. The grouping into sets, the tone-mark and
vowel-letter comparison that decides which words belong together, and the
frequency-based tiering are new, computed from the definitions rather than
drawn from any source.

# Bare pairs

English Wiktionary, under CC BY-SA 4.0 as above: the definitions of
Vietnamese words of any syllable count (proper nouns excluded), regrouped by
their bare skeleton — every tone mark and vowel-quality letter stripped, so
may/mây/mấy/mầy or an/ăn/ân collapse to the same typed string. Where minimal
pairs isolate a single mark, bare pairs strip every mark at once, closer to
what a learner unfamiliar with Vietnamese diacritics would type on a plain
keyboard.

Changed: nothing about the definitions themselves — every gloss ships
unedited and unabridged. The skeleton grouping, the cut that drops sets
already reachable through the shipped minimal-pairs deck, and the
frequency-based tiering are new, computed from the definitions rather than
drawn from any source.

# Emoji names

Unicode CLDR's emoji annotations: the Vietnamese and English name of each
emoji, and its search keywords. This is the same data that names emoji in
Android, iOS and most keyboards. The required notice is at the end of this
page.

https://github.com/unicode-org/cldr

Unicode's emoji-test.txt, for which family each emoji belongs to — faces,
animals, food, flags — and for which spelling of a sequence is the canonical
one.

https://www.unicode.org/Public/emoji/

Changed: the two languages paired, and an emoji kept only where both name it;
emoji that depict marks rather than things — arrows, punctuation, keycaps —
dropped, along with skin-tone variants, which CLDR names by suffixing the
base emoji's name. Country flag names come from CLDR's derived annotations,
which generate them from its territory names; no other derived name is used.

The emoji themselves are drawn from Noto Emoji and Noto Color Emoji, by
Google, under the SIL Open Font License 1.1. Both are subset to the glyphs
this app shows. Neither declares a Reserved Font Name. The licences ship with
the fonts, in data/fonts/OFL.txt.

https://fonts.google.com/noto/specimen/Noto+Emoji
https://openfontlicense.org

An emoji card also asks Google Fonts for a colour version of the one emoji it
is showing, because the bundled colour font is far too large to ship whole and
the bundled monochrome one reads poorly for some emoji. This is the only
request this app makes to anyone else's server, and it tells Google your IP
address and which emoji is on screen. It is not required: if it fails, is
blocked, or you are offline, the card falls back to the fonts bundled here and
nothing is lost but the colour.

https://developers.google.com/fonts/faq/privacy

# English translations

The English translations of the verse and prose above were produced by Claude
(Anthropic), translating the Vietnamese texts in this repository directly. No
published English translation was used as a source.

They have not been checked by a human Vietnamese speaker. Treat them as a
study aid, not as authoritative or scholarly translations. Each data file
records how and when it was translated.

# Word glosses

The "words" breakdown on a verse or prose card — the contextual meaning of a
word in the passage in front of you, with its literal reading, its parts and a
note where one helps — was composed for this repository by Claude (Anthropic),
reading each passage directly.

It is not derived from Wiktionary, and it is not derived from any published
commentary, annotated edition or study edition. Annotated editions of Truyện
Kiều are a real scholarly genre with real copyrights; nothing in this pipeline
is sourced from one, and none was consulted.

Like the translations above, these glosses have not been checked by a human
Vietnamese speaker. Where scholarship genuinely differs on a reading, the
entry says so rather than presenting a confident gloss; where an entry does
not say so, that is the model's judgement and not a scholarly consensus. Treat
them as a study aid.

Glosses are being added one work at a time, so most of the corpus does not
carry them yet. What ships today is the whole of Truyện Kiều — all 1,627
couplets of it, the longest work in the corpus to carry glosses end to end —
the whole of Lục Vân Tiên, all 1,041 couplets, the whole of Số đỏ, all 1,180
records, the whole of the Universal
Declaration of Human Rights, the whole of Chí Phèo, all 238 records, the whole
of Hà Nội băm sáu phố phường, all 306 records, the whole of Lão Hạc, all
77 records, the whole of Gió lạnh đầu mùa, all 63 records, the whole of
Nửa chừng xuân, all 1,351 records, the whole of Dưới bóng hoàng lan, all
45 records, and the whole of Sống mòn, all 1,616 records and the largest work
in the corpus. How they are produced, and what the pilot found, is recorded in
dataraw/gloss/.
